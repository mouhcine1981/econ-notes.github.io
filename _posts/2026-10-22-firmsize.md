The first post in this series asked about the age of the firms where North Carolinians work. Here the question is about the distribution of workers by firm size, or more specifically how many people work for a handful of large employers, and how many work for many small ones. A county's workforce can potentially be concentrated in young firms that are also large, or in old firms that stay small. Age and size don't necessarily move in the same direction. The first post looked at entry and survival and this one looks at where North Carolina's workers actually are: concentrated in a few large payrolls, spread across many small ones, or some mix that differs by place and by industry.

## Why Firm Size Matters

A workforce concentrated among a few large employers behaves differently from one spread across many small ones, even at the same total employment level. Workers at large firms tend to be paid more and are more likely to be offered benefits such as health insurance. Workers at small firms see jobs created and destroyed faster, and are more exposed to disruptions at a single firm, but small firms collectively account for a large share of net new hiring in a healthy economy.

How a county experiences a downturn or an upswing also depends in part on this distribution. A high concentration of workers in a small number of firms ties a county's fortunes closely to those firms: if one relocates, automates, or struggles, a large share of the county's workers feel it at once, and that isn't easily offset by a single decision. Where workers are spread across many small employers, no single firm's fortunes carry that much weight for the county as a whole, but the tradeoff is fewer of the higher-paying, more stable jobs that larger employers are more likely to offer.

This distribution also shapes which policy levers apply to a place. Small-business lending, SBA guarantees, and local entrepreneurship programs reach workers at firms in the smallest size categories here. Site-selection incentives and large-employer retention deals reach workers at the other end. Knowing how a county's workforce splits across that spectrum is a prerequisite for knowing which of those tools apply to it.

## How Do We Assess This Question?

Using Census Bureau Quarterly Workforce Indicators (QWI) data, we can break down private-sector employment in every North Carolina county by the size of the employing firm, in five buckets: 0-19 employees, 20-49 employees, 50-249 employees, 250-499 employees, and 500 or more employees. That gives us, for any county, industry, or year, the share of workers who fall into each bucket. As with firm age, that breakdown lets us ask three questions:

1. **Does the distribution of workers by firm size differ by industry?** Are a sector's workers concentrated at small operators, or spread across a handful of large firms?
2. **Does it differ by county?** Is the workforce split between small and large employers evenly across the state, or concentrated in particular places?
3. **How has it changed over time?** Are North Carolina's workers consolidating into larger firms, spreading into smaller ones, or holding steady?

We again pay particular attention to the three-county Cape Fear region, New Hanover, Brunswick, and Pender, both because it was the focus of the first post and because it lets us ask whether the same counties that stood out for firm age also stand out for firm size, or whether those turn out to be two different stories about the same place.

## What Do We Find?

Firm size varies by industry roughly as much as firm age does, and for the most part the two track together: industries where workers are mostly at young firms tend to be industries where workers are mostly at small firms, since a firm that hasn't been operating long has usually not had time to grow large. Agriculture, Forestry, Fishing & Hunting and Other Services (Except Public Admin) have the largest share of their workers at the smallest firms, 56 and 46 percent respectively at firms with fewer than 20 employees, and Other Services was also one of the two youngest industries in the first post. At the other end, Utilities and Management of Companies have 93 and 89 percent of their workers at firms with 500 or more employees, matching their position as two of the three oldest industries by firm age.

The relationship breaks down for Accommodation & Food Services, the other industry the first post flagged as youngest by firm age. Only 20 percent of its workers are at firms with fewer than 20 employees, well below Agriculture or Other Services, and 42 percent are at firms with 250 or more employees. A young firm in this industry is not necessarily a small one: individual restaurant and hotel locations may turn over quickly, but a sizable share of that turnover happens inside operations large enough to employ hundreds of people. Whether that means multi-unit operators, chains, or institutional food service, firm age and firm size are measuring two different things here, not the same thing twice.

Geographically, the picture is more consistent. Among the three Cape Fear counties, 35 percent of Pender's workers are at firms with fewer than 20 employees, compared with 28 percent in Brunswick and 21 percent in New Hanover, close to the statewide figure. That ordering, Pender highest, Brunswick second, New Hanover closer to the state as a whole, is the same ordering the first post found for young-firm employment share. It holds for every one of the five firm-size categories, not just the smallest: Pender and Brunswick stay above the state median on every category through 50-249 employees, while New Hanover is the only one of the three above the median at 500 or more employees, and Pender is the lowest of the three there. The three counties sit next to each other on a map, but they occupy three different points on the same small-to-large spectrum, consistently, across every cut of the data we have.

Since 2015, the share of workers at small firms has edged down in all three counties and statewide: from 24 to 21 percent in New Hanover, 31 to 28 percent in Brunswick, 39 to 35 percent in Pender, and 17 to 16 percent statewide. The ranking among the three never changes over the decade. Taken at face value, that looks like a region drifting, modestly, toward larger employers.

The raw counts show why the share slipped without small firms actually losing ground. Employment at small firms across the three-county region grew the entire decade, from about 33,000 jobs in 2015 to about 41,200 in 2025, a gain of roughly 25 percent. But firms with 500 or more employees grew more than twice as fast, from about 45,400 to about 70,500, a gain of roughly 55 percent, and every other category in between grew faster than the smallest one too. None of the five categories shrank. The small-firm share fell, from about 27 to about 24 percent of the region's total, not because small employers lost jobs but because the region's growth over the decade concentrated more in its larger employers than its smaller ones.
