# Young Firms, Old Firms: What Firm Age Tells Us About North Carolina's Economy



---

Every business in North Carolina was a new business once. There is considerable variation across communities in the types of firms that operate there and the type of activities they engage in. How old a firm is carries significant economic information as young firms create jobs differently than old firms, fail differently, pay differently, and cluster in different places.

This post is the first in a series digging into the structure of North Carolina's employers, specifically how many jobs exist and what kind of firms are providing them. We're starting with firm age and will examine in later posts firm size, and worker characteristics.

## Why Firm Age Matters

Young firm creation is typically associated with optimism about a region's prospects. Young firms also tend to be more nimble, which means they are more likely to expand quickly, and also far more likely to fail within their first few years. More mature companies are potentially less dynamic but also more stable.

That distinction matters when we think about regional economic policy. A county full of young firms looks different, and needs different things, than a county dominated by long-established employers. Young-firm-heavy economies tend to need more support around capital access and survival in those fragile early years. Mature-firm-heavy economies tend to be more stable, but can also be more vulnerable to disruption if one or two long-standing anchor employers falter.

## How Do We Assess This Question?

Using Census Bureau Quarterly Workforce Indicators (QWI) data, we can break down private-sector employment in every North Carolina county by exactly how old the employing firm is, in five buckets: 0-1 years, 2-3 years, 4-5 years, 6-10 years, and 11 or more years, which lets us examine three separate questions:

1. **Does firm age differ by industry?** Are certain sectors, restaurants, tech, construction, naturally younger than others?
2. **Does firm age differ by county?** Is the mix of young and old employers evenly spread across the state, or concentrated in particular places?
3. **How has this changed over time?** Is North Carolina's business base getting younger, older, or holding steady?

We pay particular attention throughout to the three-county Cape Fear region, New Hanover, Brunswick, and Pender, since it's one of the fastest-growing corners of the state and a useful lens for the rest of this series.

## What We're Finding

Firm age varies sharply by industry. Accommodation & Food Services and Other Services (Except Public Admin) have the youngest employment base in the state: only 67-68 percent of their workers are at firms 11 years or older, versus 93-97 percent at Finance & Insurance, Management of Companies, and Utilities. Restaurants, bars, salons, and repair shops churn through new entrants constantly; banks, holding companies, and power utilities almost never do.

![Firm age mix by industry, North Carolina 2025]({{ '/assets/images/F1_firm_age_by_industry.png' | relative_url }})

Geographically, young-firm employment is not evenly spread across North Carolina's 100 counties, and the Cape Fear region stands out. Pender County ranks among the very highest in the state for young-firm (0-3 year) employment share, trailing only Graham County statewide, with Brunswick also landing in the top 10. New Hanover sits further down the ranking but still above the statewide median. The county map makes the regional clustering visible at a glance: outside of a handful of small, idiosyncratic counties in the mountains and northeast, the Cape Fear region is one of the most consistently young-firm-heavy pockets of the state.

![Where young firms are concentrated across North Carolina counties, 2025]({{ '/assets/images/F2_young_firm_county_map.png' | relative_url }})

![Young-firm employment share ranked by county, 2025]({{ '/assets/images/F3_young_firm_county_rank.png' | relative_url }})

That gap holds up over time, not just in a single snapshot. Since 2015, young firms have consistently accounted for a larger share of employment in the Cape Fear region than in the rest of North Carolina, by roughly 3 to 4 percentage points in every year on record. Both series dipped around the pandemic, but the Cape Fear region pulled further ahead on the recovery: by 2025, young firms make up close to 11 percent of private employment in the three-county region, compared with about 6 percent in the rest of the state, the widest the gap has been in the decade we observe.
