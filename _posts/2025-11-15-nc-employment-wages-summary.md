Employment and wages in North Carolina have both grown since 2000, but not at the same pace, and not for the same reasons. Nominal wages are up 121% since 2000. Strip out inflation, though, and real wage growth is a much more modest 21%, actually trailing the state's 27% growth in total employment over the same period.

## The state's pay gap is stark

Trade, Transportation & Utilities is North Carolina's largest employer, 931,367 workers, but pays an average of just $58,082 a year. Compare that to Information sector jobs, which average $130,517, or Financial Activities at $115,747. The catch: those higher-paying sectors employ a fraction of the workforce. Most North Carolinians still work in the state's larger, lower-paying traditional sectors, which is a big part of why real wage growth has felt so uneven on the ground even as the state's headline economy expands.

## Jobs are concentrated in a shrinking number of counties

The top 10 counties now account for more than 70% of all employment in North Carolina, up from 52% in 2000. Mecklenburg County alone makes up over 20% of statewide jobs. Most rural counties, by contrast, offer fewer than 200 jobs per 1,000 residents.

![Employment growth by county, North Carolina]({{ '/assets/images/A1_nc_county_emp_growth_map.png' | relative_url }})

That divide is exactly what agglomeration economics would predict: strong urban job centers keep getting more attractive to employers, which widens the gap with rural counties further still.

## Wilmington's three counties are diverging

Locally, New Hanover remains the region's clear economic anchor, 70% of metro jobs, 74% of wages, 64% of business establishments. But Brunswick and Pender are the growth story: Brunswick's employment is up 88% since 2000, nearly doubling. That growth is concentrated in construction and leisure/hospitality, both lower-wage sectors, which is worth keeping in mind when reading the county's headline growth numbers.

## Why it matters

Leisure & hospitality and construction remain the industries actually driving job growth in this region, and both are lower-paying, slower-growing-in-real-terms sectors. That combination, strong headline job growth, weaker real wage growth, helps explain a pattern a lot of residents are already feeling: things look good on paper, but paychecks aren't stretching as far as the growth numbers might suggest.
