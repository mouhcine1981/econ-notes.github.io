In this post, we describe the socioeconomic changes that have occurred in the Cape Fear region and in North Carolina as a whole, using the most recent release of the American Community Survey. The 2025 one-year American Community Survey (ACS) provides the most recent detailed picture of who lives in each county, what they earn, what they pay for housing, how they get to work, and where they lived a year earlier. We use it to ask how the three counties of the Cape Fear region, New Hanover, Brunswick, and Pender, have changed since 2019, and whether those changes have moved the region closer to or further from the rest of North Carolina.

## Why This Matters

The Cape Fear region has been among the faster-growing parts of North Carolina for more than a decade, however growth on its own says relatively little about how a place is changing. Two counties can add the same number of residents, while one adds retirees who own their homes outright and the other adds young families who rent and commute to jobs in a neighboring county. These two kinds of growth have very different implications for schools, health care, roads, the housing market, and the local labor force.

In addition, since 2019 there have been considerable shocks that could plausibly have altered the character of the region's growth. The shift to remote work made it possible for some workers to live far from their employers, and coastal counties with relatively high amenities are the kind of place we might expect them to choose. Home prices and rents rose quickly across the country after 2020, which raises the question of whether incomes in the region kept pace. Perhaps most importantly, it is important to know whether the region's experience is unique or whether it simply mirrors what happened in the state as a whole. The answer to that question helps determine whether local policy needs to respond to something specific about the region or to broader trends that no single county can influence.

## How Do We Assess This Question?

We rely primarily on the ACS one-year estimates for 2019 and for 2021 through 2025. These are the most current ACS figures available which have a few important limitations. First, they are only published for counties with 65,000 or more residents, which in North Carolina covers 44 of the 100 counties in 2025. New Hanover and Brunswick are included in every year, while Pender is missing in 2019 and 2021. Second, there is no standard one-year release for 2020, because the Census Bureau did not publish one after the pandemic disrupted data collection, so the change between 2019 and 2021 in every trend chart covers two years rather than one. Third, all ACS figures are estimates from a sample and carry margins of error.

To understand who is moving, we use two ACS tables that report migration by age. The first counts people who lived in a different county or state, or abroad, one year earlier, and the second counts people who lived in the county one year earlier and have since moved to another county or state. Together they allow us to calculate, for each age group, how many people moved in, how many moved out, and the difference between the two, which we refer to as net domestic migration. We express each of these as a rate per 1,000 residents of the same age group. For North Carolina, these rates include moves between counties within the state, so the state figure can be read as the rate for an average county. These tables have varying coverage across counties and years. They cover New Hanover from 2019 through 2025, but Brunswick only through 2023, and Pender in no year, so the 2025 comparisons below are for New Hanover only. We also use the Census Bureau's annual county population estimates, which measure the population on July 1 of each year, to divide population growth into births minus deaths, international migration, and domestic migration.

In this next paragraph, we describe a few other decisions we make to facilitate the comparisons and the interpretation of the data. All dollar values are expressed in 2025 dollars using the Consumer Price Index, so that a change in income or rent reflects a change in purchasing power rather than inflation. To compare the Cape Fear region with the rest of the state, we group counties into a few different regions which are the Cape Fear region itself, the Triangle, the Charlotte area, the Triad, the Asheville area, the other counties on the coast covered by the Coastal Area Management Act, and the rest of the state. An important caveat is that a county within these regions is only included if it has a one-year estimate in both years. Therefore,  each region includes only its counties with a one-year estimate in both 2019 and 2025. For the Cape Fear region, this means that the 2019 to 2025 regional comparisons cover New Hanover and Brunswick only. For shares, such as the poverty rate, the regional figure is calculated exactly by adding up the counties. For medians, such as median household income, this is not possible, and the regional figure is instead an average of the county medians weighted by the number of households, owners, renters, or residents.

## What Do We Find?

### Population Growth and Where It Came From

The Cape Fear region continued to grow faster than the rest of North Carolina, although the three counties grew at very different rates. Between 2019 and 2025, the population of Brunswick grew by 22.3 percent, second only to Johnston among the 41 counties with a one-year estimate in both years, while the population of New Hanover grew by 4.9 percent, compared with 6.8 percent for the state as a whole. In the Census Bureau's population estimates, which run from July 2020 to July 2025, the region grew by 15.9 percent, more than any other region and well above the Triangle (11.1 percent) and the Charlotte area (10.2 percent). In these estimates, New Hanover grew by 8.6 percent, somewhat faster than the state's 7.2 percent.

![Population growth since 2019, North Carolina counties]({{ '/assets/images/01_population_index.png' | relative_url }})

![Population growth by region of North Carolina, July 2020 to July 2025]({{ '/assets/images/90_pep_population_index_regions.png' | relative_url }})

What distinguishes the region is not only how fast it grew but where that growth came from. In the Triangle and the Charlotte area, a sizable share of growth comes from births exceeding deaths and from international migration. In the Cape Fear region, deaths exceeded births over the five years, by about 5,400, so that all of its growth came from people moving in from elsewhere in the United States. Net domestic migration added the equivalent of 16.0 percent of the region's 2020 population, compared with 4.5 percent for the state. Brunswick is the most pronounced case, with domestic migration adding 28.9 percent to its population while natural change subtracted 3.1 percent.

![Where population growth came from, July 2020 to July 2025]({{ '/assets/images/92_pep_components_2020_2025.png' | relative_url }})

![Population change in the Cape Fear region by year and source]({{ '/assets/images/93_pep_components_by_year_cape_fear.png' | relative_url }})

The pace of in-migration has not been constant. Net domestic migration into the region peaked in the year ending July 2022, at about 16,000 people, and has since slowed, with the most recent year adding about 12,700. A few different explanations are possible, including rising housing costs, the end of the most intense period of pandemic-era relocation, and changes in remote work, and the data in this post do not allow us to separate them.

### Who Is Moving In and Who Is Moving Out

The ACS allows us to go a step further and ask whether the people moving into the region are younger or older than the people already living there. In 2025, 56 percent of the people who moved into New Hanover from another U.S. county were between 18 and 34, compared with 26 percent of its residents, which reflects the presence of UNC Wilmington and a young workforce. Movers are younger than residents everywhere, however New Hanover's net gain of 18 to 24 year olds, at 151 per 1,000 residents of that age, is far above the state rate of 5. Brunswick, unsurprisingly, is a completely different case. In 2023, the most recent year available for the county, 49 percent of those moving in from another U.S. county were 55 or older, compared with 15 percent of movers statewide. This is close to the share among Brunswick's existing residents (53 percent), so in-migration is not pulling the county's age structure younger the way it does in most counties. Put differently, migration is keeping Brunswick old, while it is keeping New Hanover younger than it would otherwise be.

![Age of people moving in and out, 2019 and 2025: New Hanover, Brunswick, Pender, and North Carolina]({{ '/assets/images/112_age_of_movers_cape_fear_2019_2025.png' | relative_url }})

 In 2023, Brunswick gained 117 people aged 55 to 64 and 17 people aged 65 to 74 per 1,000 residents of those ages through net domestic migration, compared with 10 and 8 for the average North Carolina county. New Hanover, on the other hand, gained 71 people aged 25 to 34 per 1,000 in 2025, well above the state rate of 6. It is important to note that these single-year rates for a single county carry large margins of error, and we therefore place more weight on patterns that hold in more than one year than on the size of any individual bar.

![Migration by age, 2019 and 2025: New Hanover, Brunswick, Pender, and North Carolina]({{ '/assets/images/110_migration_by_age_cape_fear_2019_2025.png' | relative_url }})

![Migration by age relative to North Carolina, 2019 and 2025]({{ '/assets/images/111_migration_by_age_cape_fear_vs_state_2019_2025.png' | relative_url }})

The full series from 2019 shows whether this pattern has changed over time. Net in-migration of residents aged 55 and over into Brunswick rose from 34 per 1,000 in 2019 to 57 in 2023, while net migration of residents aged 25 to 34 into New Hanover went from a loss of 53 per 1,000 in 2019 to a gain of 71 in 2025. The comparison of 2023 with 2025 for New Hanover tells a similar story, with net in-migration of 25 to 34 year olds rising from 13 to 71 per 1,000, while net migration of 65 to 74 year olds fell from a gain of 27 to a small loss.

![Net domestic migration by age, 2019 to 2025: New Hanover, Brunswick, Pender, and North Carolina]({{ '/assets/images/113_net_migration_by_age_series_cape_fear.png' | relative_url }})

![Age of people moving in and out, 2023 and 2025: New Hanover, Brunswick, Pender, and North Carolina]({{ '/assets/images/112_age_of_movers_cape_fear_2023_2025.png' | relative_url }})

The share of residents who had lived in another state one year earlier offers a simpler summary. In 2025, 2.7 percent of Brunswick residents and 4.0 percent of New Hanover residents had moved from another state within the past year, compared with 2.8 percent statewide. This measure moves considerably from year to year in a county the size of Brunswick, where it was 5.8 percent in 2023, which is another reason to read any single year with caution.

![Residents who lived in a different state one year earlier, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/13a_in_migration_other_state.png' | relative_url }})

### Population Change by Age

Migration by age accumulates over time into a change in the age structure of the population. Between 2019 and 2025, the number of residents aged 65 and over grew by 39 percent in Brunswick and 25 percent in New Hanover, compared with 22 percent statewide, while the number of residents under 18 grew by 12.3 percent in Brunswick and fell by 3.4 percent in New Hanover. Relative to the state, Brunswick's growth was concentrated among residents 65 and over. Perhaps more surprisingly, so was New Hanover's: residents 65 and over account for about 10,500 of the county's roughly 11,500 additional residents, despite the inflow of young adults described above.

![Population change by age, 2019 to 2025: New Hanover, Brunswick, Pender, and North Carolina]({{ '/assets/images/114_population_change_by_age_cape_fear_2019_2025.png' | relative_url }})

![Population change by age relative to North Carolina, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/115_population_change_by_age_cape_fear_vs_state_2019_2025.png' | relative_url }})

![Residents by age, 2019 to 2025: New Hanover, Brunswick, Pender, and North Carolina]({{ '/assets/images/116_population_by_age_series_cape_fear.png' | relative_url }})

As a result, residents 65 and over made up 37 percent of Brunswick's population in 2025, up from 33 percent in 2019, and 22 percent of New Hanover's, up from 18 percent.

The population pyramids and the median age summarize the result. The median age in Brunswick was 58.3 years in 2025, the highest of any county with a one-year estimate, compared with 41.0 in New Hanover and 39.6 statewide, and it rose by 2.2 years in Brunswick since 2019, compared with 0.5 years for the state.

### Incomes

Median household income was $84,349 in New Hanover, $84,019 in Brunswick, and $87,684 in Pender in 2025, compared with $77,638 statewide. All three counties are above the state figure, however the margins of error (about $5,000 to $7,000 for each county) are large enough that we would not distinguish the three counties from one another with any confidence.

![Median household income by county, 2025]({{ '/assets/images/20_dot_income.png' | relative_url }})

![Median household income, 2019 to 2025 (in 2025 dollars)]({{ '/assets/images/02_median_income_real.png' | relative_url }})

Since 2019, real median household income rose by 18.8 percent in New Hanover and 4.8 percent in Brunswick, while the state as a whole saw an increase of 7.5 percent. We urge caution in reading these changes as changes in the circumstances of long-time residents for two reasons. First, in a county that grows mainly through in-migration, the median can rise simply because the households moving in have higher incomes than those already there. Second, the in-migration of retirees, who may have substantial wealth but modest measured income, can push the median in the opposite direction. The ACS does not allow us to separate these two effects, and the age composition of migration described above suggests that both are likely at work in Brunswick.

![Change in median household income, 2019 to 2025 (in 2025 dollars)]({{ '/assets/images/30_dumbbell_income.png' | relative_url }})

![Growth in median household income relative to North Carolina, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/70_map_rel_income.png' | relative_url }})

### Housing Costs and Affordability

Housing costs in the region remain well above the state, however, in percentage terms, home values there rose more slowly than in North Carolina as a whole. The median value of owner-occupied homes rose by 32 percent in New Hanover and by 31 percent in Brunswick between 2019 and 2025 after adjusting for inflation, compared with 41 percent statewide. Rents tell a more mixed story. Median gross rent rose by 18 percent in New Hanover, slightly less than the state's 20 percent, while in Brunswick it rose by 32 percent.

![Median value of owner-occupied homes, 2019 to 2025 (in 2025 dollars)]({{ '/assets/images/03a_home_value_real.png' | relative_url }})

![Median gross rent, 2019 to 2025 (in 2025 dollars)]({{ '/assets/images/03b_rent_real.png' | relative_url }})

Because home values rose faster than incomes, the ratio of the median home value to median household income increased from 4.8 to 5.3 in New Hanover and from 3.8 to 4.7 in Brunswick, while the state ratio went from 3.4 to 4.4. The typical home in New Hanover is now worth about 5.3 years of the typical household's income, among the highest ratios in the state. The share of renters spending 30 percent or more of their income on rent was 55 percent in New Hanover in 2025, above the state figure of 50 percent, and 62 percent in Brunswick, the second-highest share of any county with a one-year estimate.

![Median home value relative to median household income, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/04_value_to_income.png' | relative_url }})

![Renters paying 30 percent or more of income on rent, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/05a_rent_burden.png' | relative_url }})

The increases were not confined to the three counties, however we see little evidence that prices spilled over into the surrounding area. Onslow is the only neighboring county with a one-year estimate in both years, and its home values rose by 29 percent, more slowly than the state. The other neighbors, Columbus, Bladen, Duplin, and Sampson, are too small for the one-year data, so we cannot say much about them here.

![Change in median home value, Cape Fear region and neighbors, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/72_map_cape_fear_chg_home_value.png' | relative_url }})

![Growth in median home value relative to North Carolina, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/70_map_rel_home_value.png' | relative_url }})

It is important to account for vacancy rates when thinking about housing figures. In 2025, 29 percent of housing units in Brunswick were vacant, compared with 12 percent statewide, and many of these are likely seasonal or second homes rather than homes for sale or rent. A large stock of seasonal homes can push up home values without adding to the supply of housing available to year-round residents.

![Share of housing units that are vacant, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/12_vacancy_rate.png' | relative_url }})

### Work From Home and Commuting

The share of workers who work from home rose sharply between 2019 and 2021 everywhere in the state, and for North Carolina as a whole it has since declined somewhat, from 18.8 percent in 2021 to 15.1 percent in 2025. This indicates that the remote work boom at the state level has considerably weakened. The Cape Fear region did not follow this pattern as the share kept rising in New Hanover, from 17.8 percent in 2021 to 22.5 percent in 2025, and in Brunswick, from 14.3 percent to 22.5 percent, so that among the 38 counties with an estimate in 2025, only Wake had a higher share. Pender, at 13.5 percent, is below the state. Across North Carolina counties, the share working from home rises with median household income, and New Hanover and Brunswick sit further above what their incomes alone would predict than any other county, by 7.5 to 8 percentage points.

![Share of workers who work from home, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/06_work_from_home.png' | relative_url }})

![Work from home and household income across North Carolina counties, 2025]({{ '/assets/images/60_scatter_wfh_income.png' | relative_url }})

Mean travel time to work for those who do not work from home was 31.9 minutes in Pender in 2025, the second-longest of any county with a one-year estimate, compared with 25.5 minutes in Brunswick, 21.5 minutes in New Hanover, and 25.7 minutes statewide. The long commutes in Pender, which has risen steadily from 27.4 minutes in 2022, are consistent with a sizable share of its working residents holding jobs in New Hanover, and with housing costs pushing households outward from Wilmington. Brunswick's commutes, by contrast, are close to the state average, which may in part reflect the strong pull of the nearby New Hanover labor market and how many of its workers do not commute at all.

![Mean travel time to work, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/07_mean_commute.png' | relative_url }})

### The Labor Market

Labor force participation is substantially lower in Brunswick than in the rest of the region and the state, at 49.8 percent in 2025 compared with 63.0 percent statewide, the lowest of any county with a one-year estimate. This largely reflects the age composition of the county rather than weak demand for workers, since retirees are counted in the population aged 16 and over but not in the labor force, and the migration figures above show that the county continues to attract residents near or past retirement age. The employment-population ratio tells a similar story.

![Labor force participation rate, residents 16 and over, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/08a_lfpr.png' | relative_url }})

![Employment-population ratio, residents 16 and over, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/08c_employment_population.png' | relative_url }})

### Education

The share of adults 25 and over with at least a bachelor's degree was 50 percent in New Hanover in 2025, well above the state figure of 38 percent, while the share in Brunswick was 44 percent. Since 2019, the share has risen by 6.7 percentage points in New Hanover and 11.2 points in Brunswick, compared with 5.8 points statewide. About six points of Brunswick's increase came in a single year, between 2024 and 2025 which potentially reflects the influence of the in-migrants.

![Adults 25 and over with a bachelor's degree or more, 2019 to 2025]({{ '/assets/images/09_bachelors_share.png' | relative_url }})

### The Cape Fear Region Compared With the Rest of the State

In this section, we bring all metrics together to ask whether the region has become more or less like the rest of North Carolina. The first two charts compare each region in 2019 and 2025, and the next two show each region's distance from the state in both years, so that an arrow moving away from the vertical line means the region has become more different from the state.

![How the Cape Fear region compares with other parts of North Carolina: incomes, housing costs, and age]({{ '/assets/images/81b_regions_change_levels.png' | relative_url }})

There are a few important takeaways worth discussing. First, the gap between the Cape Fear region and the state in home values narrowed, from 33 percent above the state in 2019 to 23 percent above it in 2025, as values in the rest of North Carolina caught up. The Triangle and the Asheville area saw a similar narrowing. Second, the region's median age rose further above the state's, while the Triangle and the Charlotte area became no older relative to the state. Third, on education and income, the region moved closer to the Triangle, which remains the region with the highest incomes and the most educated adult population. The gap in median household income between the two regions fell from about 30 to 21 percentage points relative to the state, and the gap in the bachelor's degree share fell from 10.2 to 8.6 points.

The year-by-year charts show how the gap between each county and the state evolved while the dashed line in each panel adds the three counties together in the years in which all three have a one-year estimate.

![New Hanover, Brunswick, and Pender relative to North Carolina, 2019 to 2025: incomes and housing costs]({{ '/assets/images/85b_cape_fear_gap_to_state_over_time_levels.png' | relative_url }})

We also present the same picture in ranking format and find that among the 41 counties with a one-year estimate in both 2019 and 2025, New Hanover moved from 7th to 6th in median home value and from 4th to 7th in median rent, while Brunswick remained the oldest county in both years and moved from 16th to 2nd in the share of renters paying 30 percent or more of their income on rent.

![Rank of New Hanover, Brunswick, and Pender among North Carolina counties, 2019 and 2025]({{ '/assets/images/87_focus_rank_change.png' | relative_url }})

![How New Hanover, Brunswick, and Pender compare with North Carolina, 2025]({{ '/assets/images/40_scorecard_vs_state.png' | relative_url }})

Taken together, the evidence suggests that the Cape Fear region did not simply grow along with the state. It became older, higher-income, and more educated relative to North Carolina. It also held on to the shift toward working from home that the rest of the state partly reversed. Its housing remains expensive by state standards, however the gap in home values has narrowed rather than widened and the three counties did not change in the same way. Brunswick has been receiving residents who are much older than movers elsewhere in the state, which is reflected in its rising median age, low labor force participation, and large stock of seasonal homes, while New Hanover has continued to attract younger adults, which is reflected in its large net gains of 18 to 34 year olds and its high share of renters. Even so, most of New Hanover's population growth since 2019 has been among residents 65 and over.

## Some Caveats

A few caveats apply to everything above. First, the ACS is a survey, and for counties the size of Pender or Brunswick, a one year estimate can move by several percentage points through sampling error alone. This is particularly true of the migration rates by age, which divide a county's movers into eight age groups. We therefore place more weight on patterns that appear in more than one year and across several indicators than on any single year-to-year movement. Second, the absence of a 2020 estimate means that we cannot see the exact timing of changes during the first year of the pandemic. Third, the out-migration figures only count people who moved to another county or state, so people who left the region for another country are not included in our net migration rates. Fourth, regional figures for medians are weighted averages of county medians rather than true regional medians and should be read as approximations. Finally, the migration by age tables are not available for Brunswick after 2023 or for Pender in any year.
